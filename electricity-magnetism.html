\[ d\mathbf{B} = \frac{\mu_0}{4\pi}\,\frac{I\,d\mathbf{l} \times \hat{\mathbf{r}}}{r^2} \]
SymbolDefinitionUnitType

Magnetic Field Due to a Long Straight Wire
\[ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi d} \]
SymbolDefinitionUnitType
\(B\)Magnetic flux density at perpendicular distance \(d\) from the wireTVECTOR
\(\mu_0\)Permeability of free space \((= 4\pi\times10^{-7}\ \text{H m}^{-1})\)H m⁻¹CONST
\(I\)Current in the wireASCALAR
\(d\)Perpendicular distance from the wire to the field pointmSCALAR
Derivation from Biot–Savart Law
  1. Place the wire along the \(z\)-axis. Consider a small element \(dl\) at position \(z\). The field point P is at perpendicular distance \(d\) from the wire. Let \(\theta\) be the angle between \(d\mathbf{l}\) and \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\), so \(\sin\theta = d/r\) where \(r = \sqrt{z^2+d^2}\).
  2. The Biot–Savart Law gives: \[ dB = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{\sin\theta}{r^2}\,dl \]
  3. Express everything in terms of \(\theta\). From the geometry, \(r = d/\sin\theta\) and \(z = -d\cot\theta\), so \(dz = \dfrac{d}{\sin^2\theta}\,d\theta\): \[ dB = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{\sin\theta}{(d/\sin\theta)^2}\,\frac{d}{\sin^2\theta}\,d\theta = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi d}\,\sin\theta\;d\theta \]
  4. Integrate from \(\theta = 0\) to \(\theta = \pi\) for an infinitely long wire: \[ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi d}\int_0^{\pi}\sin\theta\;d\theta = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi d}\Big[-\cos\theta\Big]_0^{\pi} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi d}\,(2) \]
  5. This gives the result: \[ \boxed{B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi d}} \]
Direction given by the right-hand grip rule: thumb along current, fingers curl in direction of \(\mathbf{B}\).
Magnetic Field at the Centre of a Circular Coil
\[ B = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2r} \]
SymbolDefinitionUnitType
\(B\)Magnetic flux density at the centre of the coilTVECTOR
\(\mu_0\)Permeability of free spaceH m⁻¹CONST
\(N\)Number of turns in the coildimensionlessSCALAR
\(I\)Current through the coilASCALAR
\(r\)Radius of the circular coilmSCALAR
Derivation from Biot–Savart Law
  1. Consider a single circular loop of radius \(r\) carrying current \(I\). We want the field at the centre (point O). Every current element \(d\mathbf{l}\) lies on the loop, and the vector from \(dl\) to O has magnitude \(r\) and is perpendicular to \(d\mathbf{l}\), so \(\sin\alpha = 1\).
  2. Each element contributes: \[ dB = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi}\,\frac{dl \cdot \sin 90°}{r^2} = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi r^2}\,dl \]
  3. By symmetry, all \(d\mathbf{B}\) contributions point in the same direction (along the axis of the loop). Integrate \(dl\) around the full loop, giving a total path length of \(2\pi r\): \[ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi r^2}\int_0^{2\pi r} dl = \frac{\mu_0 I}{4\pi r^2}\,(2\pi r) \]
  4. Simplify: \[ B = \frac{\mu_0 I}{2r} \]
  5. For a coil with \(N\) turns, each turn contributes equally, so: \[ \boxed{B = \frac{\mu_0 N I}{2r}} \]
Magnetic Field Inside a Solenoid
\[ B = \mu_0 n I \]
SymbolDefinitionUnitType
\(B\)Magnetic flux density inside the solenoid (uniform near the centre)TVECTOR
\(\mu_0\)Permeability of free spaceH m⁻¹CONST
\(n\)Number of turns per unit length \((= N/L)\)m⁻¹SCALAR
\(I\)Current through the solenoidASCALAR
Derivation from Biot–Savart Law
  1. Model the solenoid as a cylinder of radius \(R\) with \(n\) turns per unit length, each carrying current \(I\). We want the field at point P on the axis.
  2. First, the on-axis field of a single loop of radius \(R\) at axial distance \(x\) from its centre (from Biot–Savart: every \(dl\) is perpendicular to \(\hat{\mathbf{r}}\), so \(|d\mathbf{l}\times\hat{\mathbf{r}}|=dl\); only the axial component \(dB_z = |dB|\cdot R/\sqrt{R^2+x^2}\) survives by symmetry; integrating \(dl\) around the circumference \(2\pi R\)): \[ B_{\text{loop}} = \frac{\mu_0 I R^2}{2(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}} \]
  3. A thin strip at position \(x\) from P of width \(dx\) contains \(n\,dx\) turns. Its contribution: \[ dB = \frac{\mu_0\, n\, I\, R^2}{2(R^2+x^2)^{3/2}}\,dx \]
  4. Substitute \(x = R\tan\varphi\), giving \(dx = R\sec^2\!\varphi\;d\varphi\) and \((R^2+x^2)^{3/2} = R^3\sec^3\!\varphi\): \[ dB = \frac{\mu_0\, n\, I}{2}\,\cos\varphi\;d\varphi \]
  5. For an infinitely long solenoid, \(\varphi\) ranges from \(-\pi/2\) to \(\pi/2\): \[ B = \frac{\mu_0 n I}{2}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2}\cos\varphi\;d\varphi = \frac{\mu_0 n I}{2}\Big[\sin\varphi\Big]_{-\pi/2}^{\;\pi/2} = \frac{\mu_0 n I}{2}\,(2) \]
  6. This gives the result: \[ \boxed{B = \mu_0\, n\, I} \]
The field is uniform and parallel to the axis everywhere inside an ideal (infinite) solenoid, and zero outside — a consequence of the symmetry and superposition.

Practice Questions

1 Electric Fields [6]

Two point charges \(Q_1 = +3.0\;\mu\text{C}\) and \(Q_2 = -5.0\;\mu\text{C}\) are separated by \(0.40\;\text{m}\) in a vacuum.

  1. Calculate the electrostatic force between the two charges. State whether it is attractive or repulsive. [2]
  2. Determine the electric field strength at the midpoint between the two charges. [2]
  3. Find the point along the line joining the two charges where the electric potential is zero. [2]

(a) \(F = \dfrac{Qq}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} = \dfrac{(3.0\times10^{-6})(5.0\times10^{-6})}{4\pi(8.85\times10^{-12})(0.40)^2} = 0.84\;\text{N}\)

Charges are unlike, so the force is attractive.

(b) At the midpoint (\(r = 0.20\;\text{m}\) from each), both fields point from \(Q_1\) toward \(Q_2\) (same direction):

\(E = \dfrac{Q_1}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} + \dfrac{|Q_2|}{4\pi\varepsilon_0 r^2} = \dfrac{(3.0+5.0)\times10^{-6}}{4\pi(8.85\times10^{-12})(0.20)^2} = 1.8\times10^{6}\;\text{V m}^{-1}\)

(c) Let the zero-potential point be at distance \(x\) from \(Q_1\) (between the charges). \(V = 0\) when:

\(\dfrac{Q_1}{x} + \dfrac{Q_2}{0.40 - x} = 0 \implies \dfrac{3.0}{x} = \dfrac{5.0}{0.40 - x}\)

\(x = 0.15\;\text{m}\) from \(Q_1\).

2 Capacitance [6]

A \(220\;\mu\text{F}\) capacitor is charged to \(12\;\text{V}\) and then discharged through a \(47\;\text{k}\Omega\) resistor.

  1. Calculate the time constant \(\tau\) of the circuit. [1]
  2. Determine the initial charge stored and the initial discharge current. [2]
  3. Calculate the voltage across the capacitor after \(15\;\text{s}\). [2]
  4. Sketch the voltage–time graph for the discharge, labelling the time constant. [1]

(a) \(\tau = RC = 47\times10^3 \times 220\times10^{-6} = 10.3\;\text{s}\)

(b) \(Q_0 = CV_0 = 220\times10^{-6}\times 12 = 2.64\times10^{-3}\;\text{C}\) (2.64 mC)

\(I_0 = V_0/R = 12\;/\;47\times10^3 = 2.55\times10^{-4}\;\text{A}\) (0.255 mA)

(c) \(V = V_0\,e^{-t/RC} = 12\,e^{-15/10.3} = 12\,e^{-1.456} = 12\times 0.233 = 2.8\;\text{V}\)

(d) Exponential decay from 12 V. At \(t = \tau = 10.3\;\text{s}\), \(V = 12/e \approx 4.4\;\text{V}\) (37% of initial). Curve approaches 0 V asymptotically.

3 Current of Electricity [5]

A copper wire of cross-sectional area \(1.5\times10^{-6}\;\text{m}^2\) carries a current of \(3.0\;\text{A}\). Copper has a free electron density of \(n = 8.5\times10^{28}\;\text{m}^{-3}\).

  1. Calculate the mean drift velocity of the electrons. [2]
  2. A second wire of the same material carries the same current but has half the cross-sectional area. State and explain how the drift velocity compares. [2]
  3. Explain why the drift velocity is very small yet the light turns on almost instantly when the switch is closed. [1]

(a) \(v = \dfrac{I}{nAq} = \dfrac{3.0}{(8.5\times10^{28})(1.5\times10^{-6})(1.6\times10^{-19})} = 1.47\times10^{-4}\;\text{m s}^{-1}\) (\(\approx 0.15\;\text{mm s}^{-1}\))

(b) The drift velocity doubles. From \(I = nAvq\), if \(A\) is halved and \(I\), \(n\), \(q\) are unchanged, \(v\) must double to maintain the same current.

(c) The electric field propagates through the wire at close to the speed of light. All free electrons throughout the wire begin drifting almost simultaneously, so current flows at the light almost instantly — the individual electrons do not need to travel from the switch to the bulb.

4 D.C. Circuits [6]

A battery of EMF \(\varepsilon = 9.0\;\text{V}\) and internal resistance \(r = 0.50\;\Omega\) is connected to two resistors: \(R_1 = 6.0\;\Omega\) and \(R_2 = 3.0\;\Omega\) in parallel.

  1. Calculate the total resistance of the external circuit. [1]
  2. Determine the current drawn from the battery. [2]
  3. Calculate the terminal potential difference of the battery. [1]
  4. Determine the power dissipated in \(R_1\). [2]

(a) \(\dfrac{1}{R_{\text{ext}}} = \dfrac{1}{6.0} + \dfrac{1}{3.0} = \dfrac{1}{2.0}\), so \(R_{\text{ext}} = 2.0\;\Omega\)

(b) \(I = \dfrac{\varepsilon}{R_{\text{ext}} + r} = \dfrac{9.0}{2.0 + 0.50} = 3.6\;\text{A}\)

(c) \(V = \varepsilon - Ir = 9.0 - 3.6(0.50) = 7.2\;\text{V}\)

(d) The p.d. across the parallel combination is \(V = 7.2\;\text{V}\).

\(P_1 = \dfrac{V^2}{R_1} = \dfrac{7.2^2}{6.0} = 8.6\;\text{W}\)

5 D.C. Circuits — Potential Divider [4]

A potential divider consists of a thermistor (resistance \(R_T\)) in series with a \(4.7\;\text{k}\Omega\) fixed resistor, connected to a \(5.0\;\text{V}\) supply. The output voltage \(V_{\text{out}}\) is taken across the fixed resistor.

  1. Write an expression for \(V_{\text{out}}\) in terms of \(R_T\). [1]
  2. When the temperature increases, the resistance of the thermistor decreases. Explain what happens to \(V_{\text{out}}\). [2]
  3. At a certain temperature, \(V_{\text{out}} = 3.0\;\text{V}\). Calculate \(R_T\). [1]

(a) \(V_{\text{out}} = 5.0 \times \dfrac{4700}{R_T + 4700}\)

(b) As temperature rises, \(R_T\) decreases. The fraction \(\dfrac{4700}{R_T + 4700}\) increases (denominator gets smaller). Therefore \(V_{\text{out}}\) increases.

(c) \(3.0 = 5.0 \times \dfrac{4700}{R_T + 4700}\)

\(R_T + 4700 = 5.0 \times 4700\;/\;3.0 = 7833\)

\(R_T = 3100\;\Omega\) (3.1 k\(\Omega\))

6 Electromagnetism [5]

A proton (\(m = 1.67\times10^{-27}\;\text{kg}\), \(q = 1.6\times10^{-19}\;\text{C}\)) enters a uniform magnetic field of flux density \(B = 0.25\;\text{T}\) at a speed of \(4.0\times10^{6}\;\text{m s}^{-1}\), perpendicular to the field.

  1. Calculate the magnetic force on the proton. [1]
  2. Explain why the proton moves in a circular path. [2]
  3. Determine the radius of the circular orbit. [2]

(a) \(F = Bqv = 0.25 \times 1.6\times10^{-19} \times 4.0\times10^6 = 1.6\times10^{-13}\;\text{N}\)

(b) The magnetic force is always perpendicular to the velocity (from \(\mathbf{F} = q\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\)). It therefore acts as a centripetal force, continuously changing the direction of the proton without changing its speed, resulting in uniform circular motion.

(c) Set magnetic force = centripetal force:

\(Bqv = \dfrac{mv^2}{r}\implies r = \dfrac{mv}{Bq} = \dfrac{1.67\times10^{-27}\times 4.0\times10^6}{0.25\times 1.6\times10^{-19}} = 0.167\;\text{m}\approx 0.17\;\text{m}\)

7 Electromagnetism — Hall Effect [4]

A Hall probe made of a semiconductor slice (thickness \(t = 0.80\;\text{mm}\), carrier density \(n = 2.5\times10^{22}\;\text{m}^{-3}\)) carries a current of \(25\;\text{mA}\) and is placed in a magnetic field of \(0.15\;\text{T}\).

  1. Calculate the Hall voltage. [2]
  2. Explain why a semiconductor is used rather than a metal for the Hall probe. [2]

(a) \(V_H = \dfrac{BI}{ntq} = \dfrac{0.15\times 25\times10^{-3}}{(2.5\times10^{22})(0.80\times10^{-3})(1.6\times10^{-19})} = \dfrac{3.75\times10^{-3}}{3.2\times10^{0}} = 1.17\times10^{-3}\;\text{V}\approx 1.2\;\text{mV}\)

(b) Semiconductors have a much lower charge carrier density \(n\) than metals (\(\sim10^{22}\) vs \(\sim10^{28}\;\text{m}^{-3}\)). Since \(V_H \propto 1/n\), a smaller \(n\) produces a much larger (and more easily measurable) Hall voltage for the same \(B\), \(I\), and \(t\).

8 Electromagnetic Induction [6]

A rectangular coil of \(200\) turns with area \(0.030\;\text{m}^2\) rotates at \(50\;\text{Hz}\) in a uniform magnetic field of \(0.12\;\text{T}\).

  1. Calculate the maximum flux linkage through the coil. [1]
  2. Determine the peak EMF induced in the coil. [2]
  3. Write an expression for the instantaneous EMF as a function of time. [1]
  4. At the instant when the EMF is at its peak value, state the value of the flux linkage through the coil. Explain your answer. [2]

(a) \(N\Phi_{\max} = NBA = 200\times 0.12\times 0.030 = 0.72\;\text{Wb}\)

(b) \(\omega = 2\pi f = 2\pi(50) = 100\pi\;\text{rad s}^{-1}\)

\(\varepsilon_0 = NBA\omega = 0.72\times 100\pi = 226\;\text{V}\approx 230\;\text{V}\)

(c) \(\varepsilon = 226\sin(100\pi t)\;\text{V}\)  (or equivalently \(\varepsilon = NBA\omega\sin(\omega t)\))

(d) The flux linkage is zero when the EMF is at its peak. This is because \(\varepsilon = -d(N\Phi)/dt\): the EMF is largest when the flux linkage is changing most rapidly, which occurs as \(N\Phi\) passes through zero (the coil plane is parallel to \(\mathbf{B}\)).

9 Electromagnetic Induction — Faraday & Lenz [4]

A straight conductor of length \(0.30\;\text{m}\) moves at a constant velocity of \(5.0\;\text{m s}^{-1}\) perpendicular to a uniform magnetic field of \(0.40\;\text{T}\).

  1. Calculate the EMF induced across the ends of the conductor. [1]
  2. The conductor is part of a closed circuit of total resistance \(0.60\;\Omega\). Calculate the current and the force required to maintain the constant velocity. [2]
  3. Using Lenz's law, explain why a force is needed to maintain the velocity. [1]

(a) \(\varepsilon = BLv = 0.40\times 0.30\times 5.0 = 0.60\;\text{V}\)

(b) \(I = \varepsilon/R = 0.60\;/\;0.60 = 1.0\;\text{A}\)

Force on the current-carrying conductor in the field: \(F = BIL = 0.40\times 1.0\times 0.30 = 0.12\;\text{N}\)

(c) By Lenz's law, the induced current flows in a direction that creates a magnetic force opposing the motion of the conductor. This opposing force would decelerate the conductor, so an external force of equal magnitude is needed to maintain constant velocity — this is a consequence of conservation of energy.

10 Alternating Currents — Transformer [5]

An ideal transformer has 400 turns in the primary coil and 2000 turns in the secondary coil. The primary is connected to a \(230\;\text{V}_{\text{rms}}\) a.c. supply. A \(5.0\;\text{k}\Omega\) resistor is connected across the secondary.

  1. Calculate the secondary voltage. [1]
  2. Calculate the current in the secondary coil and the power dissipated in the resistor. [2]
  3. Determine the current in the primary coil. [1]
  4. State one reason why a real transformer is less than 100% efficient. [1]

(a) \(\dfrac{V_s}{V_p} = \dfrac{N_s}{N_p}\implies V_s = 230\times\dfrac{2000}{400} = 1150\;\text{V}\)

(b) \(I_s = \dfrac{V_s}{R} = \dfrac{1150}{5000} = 0.23\;\text{A}\)

\(P = V_s I_s = 1150\times 0.23 = 265\;\text{W}\)

(c) For an ideal transformer, \(V_p I_p = V_s I_s\):

\(I_p = \dfrac{V_s I_s}{V_p} = \dfrac{265}{230} = 1.15\;\text{A}\)

(d) Any one of: resistive heating in the coils (\(I^2R\) losses); eddy currents in the iron core; hysteresis losses in the core; magnetic flux leakage between primary and secondary.

!

Exam Tips & Common Pitfalls

General Exam Technique
  • Data booklet first. Many equations (Coulomb's law, capacitance formulas, Biot–Savart results, Faraday's law) are provided. Know what's given so you don't waste time memorising them — focus on knowing when and how to use them.
  • Units check. Before substituting, convert everything to SI. After getting an answer, verify the units make sense dimensionally — catches sign and formula errors.
  • Vectors need direction. Whenever a question involves force, field, or velocity, state the direction explicitly (use Fleming's rules, right-hand rule, or coordinate signs). Marks are lost here every year.
  • Show working. Method marks are awarded even if the final answer is wrong. Write the formula, substitute with units, then calculate. Never jump straight to a number.
  • Significant figures. Give 2–3 s.f. unless told otherwise. Don't round intermediate steps — only round the final answer.
17 · Electric Fields
  • Common mistake: Confusing \(E\) (vector, N C\(^{-1}\)) with \(V\) (scalar, J C\(^{-1}\)). Field has direction; potential does not.
  • \(V\) due to a negative charge is negative. Don't drop the sign — it matters when summing potentials from multiple charges.
  • \(E = -dV/dr\) means \(\mathbf{E}\) points from high \(V\) to low \(V\). On a sketch, field lines are perpendicular to equipotentials and point "downhill".
  • "Work done by an external agent" and "work done by the field" are equal in magnitude but opposite in sign. Read the question carefully.
  • For two point charges: use Coulomb's law for force, sum potentials algebraically (scalars) but add fields as vectors (resolve components).
18 · Capacitance
  • Opposite to resistors: Capacitors in parallel add directly (\(C = C_1 + C_2\)); in series it's the reciprocal rule. This is the reverse of resistors — don't mix them up.
  • After \(5\tau\) the capacitor is effectively fully charged/discharged (> 99%). If a question says "a long time", treat it as \(t \to \infty\).
  • Energy stored = \(\frac{1}{2}CV^2\). The area under a \(V\)-\(Q\) graph is also the energy stored (triangle for a linear capacitor).
  • During discharge, \(Q\), \(V\), and \(I\) all decay exponentially with the same time constant \(\tau = RC\). During charging, \(Q\) and \(V\) rise but \(I\) still decays.
  • If asked to find \(\tau\) from a graph: read off the time at which the value falls to \(0.37\) (i.e. \(1/e\)) of its initial value, or rises to \(0.63\).
19 · Current of Electricity
  • Drift velocity is extremely slow (order of mm s\(^{-1}\)). The signal travels near light speed, but the electrons do not.
  • Ohm's law (\(V = IR\)) only applies to ohmic conductors at constant temperature. A filament lamp or diode is not ohmic — say so explicitly if asked.
  • \(V = \varepsilon - Ir\): as \(I\) increases, terminal p.d. \(V\) decreases. When \(I = 0\) (open circuit), \(V = \varepsilon\). When the load is short-circuited, \(V = 0\) and \(I = \varepsilon/r\).
  • Graph trick: A \(V\) vs \(I\) graph for a real battery is a straight line with y-intercept = \(\varepsilon\) and gradient = \(-r\).
  • \(P = I^2R\) is useful when current is the same (series); \(P = V^2/R\) is useful when voltage is the same (parallel).
20 · D.C. Circuits
  • Potential divider: \(V_{\text{out}} = V_{\text{in}} \times R_2/(R_1+R_2)\). Output is across \(R_2\) (the bottom resistor). If a sensor (LDR, thermistor) replaces one resistor, think about how its resistance changes and what that does to \(V_{\text{out}}\).
  • A voltmeter in parallel with a component draws some current — this lowers the p.d. it measures. An ideal voltmeter has infinite resistance.
  • Kirchhoff strategy: (1) Label all currents with directions. (2) Apply KCL at each junction. (3) Apply KVL around independent loops. (4) Solve simultaneous equations. If a current comes out negative, it simply flows opposite to your assumed direction.
  • For combined series-parallel networks: simplify from the inside out. Identify which resistors are truly in series (same current) vs truly in parallel (same p.d.).
21 · Electromagnetism
  • Fleming's Left-Hand Rule (motor effect): thuMb = Motion/Force, First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current. Use for both \(F = BIL\sin\theta\) and \(F = Bqv\sin\theta\).
  • The magnetic force on a moving charge is always perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\). It changes direction but not speed — so it does no work and the kinetic energy stays constant.
  • A charged particle in a uniform \(\mathbf{B}\) (perpendicular to \(\mathbf{v}\)) moves in a circle. Set \(Bqv = mv^2/r\) to find the radius.
  • Hall effect: \(V_H = BI/(ntq)\). Semiconductors have much smaller \(n\) than metals, so they produce a much larger Hall voltage — this is why Hall probes use semiconductor slices.
  • For the force between two parallel wires: currents in the same direction attract; opposite directions repel.
22 · Electromagnetic Induction
  • Most common error: Lenz's law says the induced current opposes the change in flux — not the flux itself. If flux is increasing, the induced current creates a field opposing the increase. If flux is decreasing, it tries to maintain it.
  • Phase trap: For a rotating coil, flux linkage is \(NBA\cos(\omega t)\) but EMF is \(NBA\omega\sin(\omega t)\). EMF is maximum when flux linkage is zero (coil parallel to \(\mathbf{B}\)), and EMF is zero when flux linkage is maximum (coil perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}\)).
  • Faraday's law: \(\varepsilon = -d(N\Phi)/dt\). The magnitude of the EMF is the gradient of the flux linkage–time graph. Steeper graph = larger EMF.
  • A straight conductor of length \(L\) moving at speed \(v\) perpendicular to \(\mathbf{B}\): the induced EMF is simply \(\varepsilon = BLv\) (a useful shortcut derived from Faraday's law).
23 · Alternating Currents
  • RMS means "equivalent d.c.". An a.c. supply of \(V_{\text{rms}} = 230\) V delivers the same average power to a resistor as a 230 V d.c. supply. Peak voltage is \(V_0 = 230\sqrt{2} \approx 325\) V.
  • \(V_{\text{rms}} = V_0/\sqrt{2}\) only applies to sinusoidal waveforms. If the waveform is square, triangular, etc., you must calculate RMS from first principles (integrate \(V^2\) over one period).
  • Transformer: Step-up voltage = step-down current (and vice versa). Power is conserved for an ideal transformer: \(V_p I_p = V_s I_s\).
  • Real transformers lose energy to: (1) resistive heating in coils (\(I^2R\) losses), (2) eddy currents in the core (reduced by lamination), (3) hysteresis losses in the core, (4) flux leakage. Know all four for "explain" questions.
  • On a CRO trace: \(V_0\) is read from the peak, and the period \(T\) from one complete cycle. Then \(f = 1/T\) and \(V_{\text{rms}} = V_0/\sqrt{2}\).
